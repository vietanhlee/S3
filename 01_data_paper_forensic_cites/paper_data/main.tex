\begin{abstract}
Rapid forensic discrimination of protected timbers at customs checkpoints requires robust macroscopic identification methods. However, previous datasets for computer-vision wood anatomy often lack multimodal descriptors, rely on uncalibrated optics, or omit heavily trafficked species regulated under CITES. We introduce ForensicMacroWood-CITES, an authenticated multimodal benchmark comprising 6,414 standardized high-resolution transverse-plane images ($12.0\,\mu\text{m/pixel}$) from 19 commercially significant Fabaceae hardwoods. The cohort encompasses 11 CITES Appendix~II-regulated taxa and 4 high-value regional commercial species, anchored to 147 verified physical wood block accessions. To bridge computer vision and classical xylotomy, the dataset integrates an expert-curated matrix of 14 macroscopic anatomical descriptors standardized under IAWA criteria, facilitating multimodal vision-language learning and explainable AI auditing. To address reference specimen scarcity, we provide a dual-partition architecture: an operational canonical benchmark guaranteeing 100\% class coverage alongside an audited specimen-disjoint split ($0.0\%$ specimen leakage across multi-specimen taxa). Technical validation using ConvNeXt-Tiny establishes reproducible baselines: $90.42\% \pm 0.38\%$ overall accuracy on the operational canonical split ($N_{\text{test}} = 1{,}190$, macro-F1: $86.80\% \pm 0.45\%$, Pairwise Specimen Leakage Rate: $30.61\%$) and $82.35\% \pm 0.52\%$ under the strict specimen-disjoint benchmark in 19-class mode ($N_{\text{test}} = 1{,}204$, macro-F1: $76.92\% \pm 0.61\%$, $0.0\%$ specimen leakage across 17 multi-specimen taxa, with 144 test captures from two single-accession taxa evaluated intra-specimen; alongside a pure 17-class zero-leakage test mode with $N_{\text{test}} = 1{,}060$, accuracy $84.10\%$, and $\text{CCR} = 89.5\%$), confirming benchmark functionality and out-of-distribution robustness. The benchmark provides a non-destructive screening testbed for timber forensics and is publicly accessible at \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.14892180}{10.5281/zenodo.14892180}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Value of the Data}
\label{sec:value}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Dedicated Forensic Reference for CITES Hardwood Compliance}: This dataset provides an authenticated benchmark for fine-grained discrimination of 19 commercially significant tropical Fabaceae hardwoods. With 11 taxa strictly regulated under CITES Appendix~II, it directly supports customs checkpoints and wildlife enforcement authorities in combatting illicit timber trafficking.
    \item \textbf{Rapid Non-Destructive Screening Testbed}: Unlike conventional forensic microtomy which is destructive and requires days per sample, these standardized transverse captures ($12.0\,\mu\text{m/pixel}$) enable rapid, non-destructive front-line triage of timber consignments within minutes.
    \item \textbf{Multimodal Ground Truth for Explainable AI}: The dataset integrates an expert-curated matrix of 14 macroscopic anatomical descriptors informed by IAWA criteria. This structural metadata supports multimodal vision-language models, visual question answering, and explainable AI audits to ensure models attend to genuine botanical features.
    \item \textbf{Dual-Split Architecture Addressing Specimen Scarcity}: To overcome acute specimen scarcity in authentic xylarium archives, the repository provides two audited partitions. The operational canonical benchmark prioritizes a 100\% Class Coverage Rate across all subsets for frontline inspection, while a strict specimen-disjoint split enables evaluation of inter-specimen biological invariance across unseen physical logs.
    \item \textbf{Turnkey Machine Learning Reproducibility}: The release delivers scale-preserving cropped tiles alongside reproducible ConvNeXt-Tiny classification baselines ($90.42\% \pm 0.38\%$ on canonical split, $82.35\% \pm 0.52\%$ on strict specimen-disjoint split across three random seeds). Provided quickstart scripts ensure immediate adoption for machine learning research.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Taxonomic Composition and Inventory}

The ForensicMacroWood-CITES repository comprises \textbf{6,414 standardized macroscopic cross-sectional captures} spanning \textbf{19 commercially significant tropical hardwood species} across \textbf{6 botanical genera} within the legume family \textbf{Fabaceae} (Leguminosae). All images were curated from institutional timber reference archives at the Intelligent Computing for Sustainable Development Laboratory (IC4SD), PTIT, Hanoi, Vietnam. Each capture carries a verified species-level label linked to traceable physical wood specimens ($|\mathcal{G}_c| = 147$ verified wood block accessions) and confirmed through an independent dual-expert verification protocol ($\kappa = 0.985$).

\subsection{Comparative Dataset Positioning}
\label{sec:comparative_positioning}

To contextualize the forensic utility of ForensicMacroWood-CITES, Table~\ref{tab:dataset_comparison} systematically compares its core attributes against prominent public macroscopic wood datasets and recent literature benchmarks~\cite{ravindran2020,ravindran2021,figueroamata2022,song2025,liu2025,nguyentrong2026eucalyptus}. Foundational initiatives like the XyloTron project~\cite{ravindran2020,ravindran2021} and regional surveys~\cite{figueroamata2022,song2025} have significantly advanced computer vision in forestry. However, these existing public collections typically focus on non-regulated commercial timbers with minimal CITES coverage ($<15\%$). They also frequently utilize lower optical magnifications or non-standardized mobile lenses. 

Recent institutional benchmarks, such as IC4SD-Wood-Eucalyptus~\cite{nguyentrong2026eucalyptus}, established rigorous image curation but focused exclusively on plantation hardwoods ($0\%$ CITES) for pulpwood management. Meanwhile, specialized studies targeting high-value CITES taxa~\cite{liu2025} remain restricted to private, unshared archives. This highlights a critical gap in open-access forensic resources for endangered tropical hardwoods.

ForensicMacroWood-CITES addresses this gap with three distinguishing contributions. First, it targets \textbf{19 commercially significant tropical hardwoods across 6 Fabaceae genera}, with a critical concentration of trade-restricted timbers (\textbf{57.9\% CITES Appendix~II}, 11 regulated species) alongside 4 high-value regional commercial species. Second, it provides an expert-curated matrix of \textbf{14 diagnostic macroscopic anatomical descriptors} structured under IAWA xylotomical standards, enabling multimodal paradigms absent in prior single-modality collections. Third, it delivers high optical calibration ($12.0\,\mu\text{m/pixel}$, $\approx 0.29\times$ optical magnification), verified physical block traceability, dual partition manifests, and audited perceptual boundaries.

\subsection{Partition Allocations and Class Distributions}

The 6,414 captures are partitioned following a governed specimen-aware allocation protocol across physical wood blocks (Training: 3,959 images, $61.72\%$; Validation: 1,265 images, $19.72\%$; Test: 1,190 images, $18.55\%$). Crucially, all 19 species achieve a 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($\text{CCR} = 100.0\%$, 19/19 taxa) across all three partitions.

Authentic timber reference archives are inherently characterized by small specimen cohorts. Across all 19 taxa, our collection contains 147 verified physical wood block accessions ($|\mathcal{G}_c| \le 10$ per species), with acute bottlenecks such as a single physical specimen accession for \textit{Dalbergia cochinchinensis} ($N=354$) and \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma} ($N=360$). Under these small-cohort constraints, dogmatic three-way whole-block isolation inevitably deprives minority CITES taxa of evaluation captures. For example, enforcing complete block isolation on a single-specimen class mathematically renders validation or test coverage impossible.

To resolve this trade-off between operational coverage and out-of-distribution evaluation, our dataset architecture provides two distinct, explicitly documented partitions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2.5pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Operational Canonical Benchmark (\texttt{splits/split\_canonical.csv})}: Designed as a practical operational baseline for customs screening. By permitting controlled specimen boundary sharing (overall Specimen Leakage Rate $\text{SLR} = 30.61\%$, calculated as 45 shared block instances across partition pairs out of 147 total blocks: 21 Train--Val, 12 Train--Test, and 12 Val--Test), this partition guarantees a 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($\text{CCR} = 100.0\%$, 19/19 taxa) across Train, Validation, and Test subsets.
    \item \textbf{Strict Specimen-Disjoint Benchmark (\texttt{splits/split\_specimen\_disjoint.csv})}: Designed for machine learning research on out-of-distribution generalization across unseen physical logs. For all 17 multi-specimen taxa ($|\mathcal{G}_c| \ge 4$, aggregating 145 physical blocks), physical wood blocks are strictly isolated across subsets with zero boundary sharing ($\text{SLR} = 0.0\%$).
\end{enumerate}

Furthermore, the variation in split ratios across individual species in Table~\ref{tab:split_allocation} is an inherent consequence of discrete block-indivisibility. Physical reference blocks from multi-specimen taxa naturally vary in cross-sectional surface area, yielding between 20 and 80 standardized tiles per block, whereas extensive cross-sectional tiling across single-accession blocks yielded larger image volumes (354 and 360 tiles). For instance, the minority class \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} ($N=116$ across 5 blocks) allocates 44 Train, 32 Val, and 40 Test captures due to discrete block sizes. Figure~\ref{fig:eda_split} illustrates per-species image volumes across partitions, and Table~\ref{tab:split_allocation} provides the comprehensive specimen and image allocation manifest across all 19 taxa.

\subsection{Diagnostic Macroscopic Anatomical Features and Multimodal Descriptors}
\label{sec:anatomical_features}

Macroscopic timber identification on transverse end-grain surfaces provides an effective diagnostic screening modality because it simultaneously reveals all primary secondary xylem tissue systems~\cite{wiedenhoeft2011}. Imaged at a standardized calibrated spatial sampling pitch of $12.0\,\mu\text{m/pixel}$ (an immutable $2.69 \times 2.69\,\text{mm}$ physical field of view per $224 \times 224$ px tile, corresponding to $\approx 0.29\times$ optical magnification onto the sensor ), taxonomic differentiation across the 19 Fabaceae species is governed by primary macroscopic morphological axes:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Vascular Porosity and Lumen Occlusion}: While the examined Fabaceae hardwoods are predominantly diffuse-porous, distinct semi-ring-porous tendencies occur in \textit{Dalbergia rimosa} and \textit{Pterocarpus erinaceus}. Vessel apertures are solitary and in short radial multiples. Vessel tangential diameters range from small pores ($90$--$160\,\mu\text{m}$ in \textit{Guibourtia} and \textit{Dalbergia melanoxylon}) to medium-to-large pores ($180$--$260\,\mu\text{m}$ in \textit{Afzelia}). In heartwood, vessels in \textit{Dalbergia} and \textit{Pterocarpus} are frequently occluded by dark organic polyphenolic deposits, whereas lumina in \textit{Afzelia} and \textit{Sindora} often contain conspicuous white crystalline deposits.
    \item \textbf{Axial Parenchyma Topography}: Axial parenchyma configuration serves as the primary taxonomic discriminator within the Leguminosae family: (i) prominent winged lozenge-aliform to confluent paratracheal sheaths forming pale halos in \textit{Afzelia}; (ii) wavy concentric tangential bands forming reticulate networks in \textit{Dalbergia} and \textit{Pterocarpus}; (iii) aliform to confluent parenchyma accompanied by marginal bands at growth boundaries in \textit{Guibourtia}; and (iv) regular continuous marginal bands demarcating growth increments in \textit{Sindora}.
    \item \textbf{Axial Intercellular Secretory Canals}: Regularly spaced axial resin canals embedded strictly within continuous marginal parenchyma bands uniquely isolate the genus \textit{Sindora} from all other examined taxa, precluding cross-genus misidentification with rosewoods or padauks.
    \item \textbf{Heartwood Chemomorphology and Coloration}: Secondary extractive deposition yields diagnostic optical contrast, including the intense purple hues of photo-oxidized peltogynoids in \textit{Peltogyne}, dark vertical pigment striping in \textit{Dalbergia} and \textit{Guibourtia ehie}, and vibrant orange-red extractives in \textit{Pterocarpus}.
\end{enumerate}

To enrich the benchmark beyond single-label classification and facilitate multimodal artificial intelligence research, the dataset incorporates an expert-curated table of structured macroscopic anatomical descriptors (\texttt{metadata/anatomical\_features.csv}). The matrix formalizes \textbf{14 distinct macroscopic anatomical and physical descriptor axes} informed by IAWA macroscopic and microscopic criteria~\cite{iawa1989} and authoritative xylotomical references~\cite{wiedenhoeft2011,insidewood}: (1) heartwood-sapwood distinction, (2) heartwood color and figure, (3) growth ring distinctness, (4) stripe figure, (5) characteristic scent, (6) vascular porosity, (7) vessel arrangement, (8) vessel size class, (9) tyloses presence, (10) vessel deposit chemomorphology, (11) axial parenchyma topography, (12) parenchyma-ray reticulation pattern, (13) storied rays (observable on tangential sections), and (14) physical air-dry density. Importantly, these descriptors represent species-level reference ground-truth attributes compiled from IAWA and InsideWood literature to guide multimodal vision-language models and concept-bottleneck audits, rather than dense per-image pixel annotations. While transverse images directly display vascular arrangement, axial parenchyma configurations, and heartwood pigmentation, three auxiliary axes (characteristic scent, storied rays observable on tangential longitudinal surfaces, and physical air-dry density) are included as multimodal domain knowledge from authoritative references. Table~\ref{tab:anatomical_features} summarizes representative profiles across key taxa.

\noindent
\textbf{Opportunities for Multimodal Vision-Language and Explainable AI}:
Because the image dataset comprises exclusively transverse end-grain captures, visual question answering (VQA) and saliency map validation (XAI) are strictly applicable to morphological features directly resolvable on the transverse plane (e.g., vessel pore grouping, axial parenchyma topography, and lumen occlusions). Non-transverse traits (such as storied rays requiring tangential sections or organoleptic aroma) serve as auxiliary multimodal semantic attributes from reference literature rather than optical targets for direct image-based inspection. Under this grounded scope, the provided descriptors unlock several advanced research paradigms:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Visual Question Answering (VQA) on Transverse Anatomical Structures}: Benchmarking vision-language models (VLMs) on domain-specific forensic inquiries restricted to transverse visual morphology (e.g., \textit{``Does this end-grain capture present winged-aliform parenchyma surrounding diffuse-porous vessels?''} or \textit{``Are lumina occluded by dark polyphenols or white crystalline deposits?''}).
    \item \textbf{Semantic Attribute-Guided and Zero-Shot Classification}: Leveraging descriptive anatomical vectors to guide species identification through biologically interpretable morphological concepts rather than opaque categorical indices, enabling zero-shot recognition of newly regulated CITES taxa.
    \item \textbf{Bidirectional Cross-Modal Retrieval}: Mapping textual forensic identification keys to macroscopic optical captures and conversely generating automated diagnostic morphological descriptions from end-grain captures (image-to-text forensic reporting).
    \item \textbf{Explainable AI (XAI) and Concept Bottleneck Models}: Serving as objective anatomical ground truth to audit whether deep vision models attend to genuine diagnostic transverse features (e.g., parenchyma wings, resin canals) or spurious surface preparation artifacts (e.g., saw marks, scratches).
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Design, Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Accurate timber species discrimination is essential for regulating global forest-product supply chains, particularly for high-value tropical hardwoods controlled under CITES Appendix~II~\cite{dormontt2015,cites}. Conventional histological microtomy---the authoritative diagnostic standard---requires prolonged softening, thin sectioning, staining, and manual IAWA feature cross-referencing, demanding specialized infrastructure and several days per sample~\cite{dormontt2015,iawa1989,wiedenhoeft2011}. Macroscopic end-grain imaging coupled with computer vision has therefore emerged as a rapid screening alternative~\cite{woodreview,ravindran2020,ravindran2022}. Our experimental workflow was engineered to guarantee optical fidelity and taxonomic authenticity under authentic reference xylarium constraints.

\subsection{Specimen Preparation and Optical Macro-Imaging}

Specimen preparation and optical surface conditioning followed standardized multi-stage laboratory procedures:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Orthogonal Transverse Surfacing}: End-grain transverse surfaces were planed strictly perpendicular to the longitudinal stem axis using an industrial sliding table saw fitted with a carbide circular blade.
    \item \textbf{Progressive Polishing}: Surfaces were smoothed sequentially on a motorized rotary platen using silicon-carbide abrasives across four calibrated grit gradations: P120, P240, P400, and P600.
    \item \textbf{Vascular Lumen De-Dusting}: Microscopic sawdust residues lodged within vessel apertures were purged using directed dry compressed-air jets at $6\,\text{bar}$ pressure, ensuring clear visibility of pore lumens and parenchyma sheaths.
    \item \textbf{Calibrated Optical Macro-Imaging Setup}: Surfaces were imaged using an industrial color CMOS sensor ($3.45\,\mu\text{m}$ pixel pitch, $1920 \times 1080$ resolution) coupled with an optical macro-lens. The system operated at a fixed working distance of $150\,\text{mm}$ and an aperture stop of $f/8.0$. This aperture balanced the optical diffraction limit against the required depth of field ($\approx 2.1\,\text{mm}$), ensuring uniform focus across the surface micro-relief.
    \item \textbf{Optical Magnification and Calibrated Sampling Pitch}: Spatial sampling pitch was calibrated against a certified optical stage micrometer, establishing an exact pitch of $12.0\,\mu\text{m}$ per pixel on the transverse specimen surface. Given the sensor's physical pixel pitch of $3.45\,\mu\text{m}$, the true optical magnification formed by the macro-lens onto the CMOS plane is $m = 3.45\,\mu\text{m} / 12.0\,\mu\text{m} \approx 0.2875\times$ ($\approx 0.29\times$ optical demagnification). This calibrated optical configuration delivers an immutable $2.69 \times 2.69\,\text{mm}$ field of view for each standardized $224 \times 224$ px tile ($7.23\,\text{mm}^2$), matching traditional stereomicroscopic diagnostic inspection scales. Illumination was provided by a daylight-balanced ($5600\,\text{K}$) diffuse LED ring light ($4500\,\text{lux}$), with white balance calibrated against an X-Rite ColorChecker card.
    \item \textbf{Scale-Preserving Non-Overlapping Tile Extraction}: Standardized $224 \times 224$ px tiles were extracted from the raw high-resolution captures via systematic, non-overlapping spatial window cropping and saved as high-quality JPEGs ($Q=95$). Crucially, dataset tiles were created by direct spatial cropping \textbf{without downsampling}, thereby preserving the physical scale of $(224 \times 12.0\,\mu\text{m}) \times (224 \times 12.0\,\mu\text{m}) \approx 2.69 \times 2.69\,\text{mm}$ ($7.23\,\text{mm}^2$). This ensures vessel pore diameters, ray widths, and parenchyma band intervals directly match physical millimeter dimensions under IAWA feature criteria. While CNN training employs mild scale jittering ($0.8$--$1.0$) as standard data augmentation against overfitting, all technical-validation inference evaluates strictly at the native, unscaled $1.0\times$ physical window ($224 \times 224$ px).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Independent Taxonomic Label Verification and Inter-Rater Reliability}
\label{sec:inter_rater}

To establish rigorous ground-truth authenticity, all physical wood specimens ($|\mathcal{G}_c| = 147$ wood blocks) and their macroscopic captures were validated through an independent, double-blind verification protocol by two certified forestry wood anatomists. Both experts independently evaluated each specimen against diagnostic wood anatomy keys from the International Association of Wood Anatomists (IAWA)~\cite{iawa1989,wiedenhoeft2011}, cross-examining vascular porosity, axial parenchyma topography (aliform wings, confluent bands, and marginal lines), ray characteristics, and secondary heartwood chemomorphology.

Inter-rater reliability between the two independent experts was quantitatively audited using both raw observed percentage agreement ($P_o$) and Cohen's kappa coefficient ($\kappa$)~\cite{cohen1960}:
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e},
\end{equation}
where $P_o$ denotes the observed proportional concordance across all 6,414 captures, and $P_e$ represents the expected chance agreement under marginal class distributions. Across the initial blind evaluation phase, the two experts achieved an observed concordance of $P_o = 98.64\%$ (6,327 of 6,414 captures identically identified at the species level) and a Cohen's kappa of $\kappa = 0.985$ (95\% CI: $[0.980, 0.990]$), denoting near-perfect diagnostic agreement. Initial ambiguities ($1.35\%$, 87 captures) were localized exclusively between congeneric sister taxa with pronounced anatomical convergence (specifically between \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} and \textit{Afzelia bella}). Disputed specimens were subsequently resolved through a joint adjudication panel with reference herbarium vouchers, supplemented by air-dry density verification and long-wave ultraviolet (UV $365\,\text{nm}$) fluorescence testing on freshly planed surfaces, establishing 100\% unanimous consensus ground-truth labeling prior to dataset release.

\subsection{Quality Control and Governed Specimen-Aware Partitioning}

To guarantee benchmark integrity and reproducibility, a rigorous quality control and governed partitioning pipeline was executed:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Focus Sharpness Filtering and Laplacian Quantification}: Across initial imaging campaigns, an initial pool of 6,680 candidate $224 \times 224$ px transverse tiles was extracted from the surfaced wood blocks. Depending on reference block availability and usable cross-sectional surface area, well-represented taxa yielded between 291 and 486 standardized captures. Optical focus sharpness was evaluated quantitatively using the variance of the discrete Laplacian operator on grayscale channels:
    \begin{equation}
    \sigma^2_{\text{Laplacian}} = \frac{1}{H \times W} \sum_{x=1}^H \sum_{y=1}^W \left( \nabla^2 I(x,y) - \mu_{\nabla^2} \right)^2.
    \end{equation}
    Tiles exhibiting $\sigma^2_{\text{Laplacian}} < 100$ were flagged as unfocused or motion-degraded and purged from the repository. This objective sharpness threshold eliminated 266 blurred tiles ($3.98\%$ of acquisitions), yielding the curated release cohort of exactly 6,414 high-quality standardized captures ($\sigma^2_{\text{Laplacian}} \ge 100$, documented per image in \texttt{metadata.csv}).
    \item \textbf{Data Integrity and Deduplication Verification}: To confirm that partitioned subsets remain completely free from exact duplicate captures or corrupted files across train, validation, and test splits, dataset integrity was audited across two standard verification protocols:
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=1.5pt]
        \item \textit{Bitwise Cryptographic Auditing (SHA-256)}: 256-bit SHA-256 cryptographic hashes were computed for every standardized image file and cross-referenced across split boundaries. In both the canonical governed partition and the strict specimen-disjoint benchmark, the number of bitwise duplicate files across splits is strictly zero ($\text{SHA-256 Overlap} = 0$, $0/6,414$ files), guaranteeing total absence of bitwise image contamination.
        \item \textit{Perceptual Structural Auditing (dHash and pHash)}: To document spatial proximity across physical wood specimens, 64-bit Difference Hash (dHash) and 64-bit DCT-based Perceptual Hash (pHash) were extracted per image and stored in \texttt{metadata.csv}. Across all $N_{\text{pairs}} = 1,190 \times 3,959 = 4,711,210$ cross-split test-vs-train comparisons in the canonical split, the mean pairwise Hamming distance is $d_H = 18.42 \pm 6.12$; while 142 cross-split image pairs exhibit $H \le 4$ (involving 26 distinct test query images, representing $26 / 1,190 = 2.18\%$ of test queries, arising exclusively from the 12 shared blocks permitting complete class coverage, with minimum Hamming distance $H_{\min} = 1$), exact perceptual duplicates ($H = 0$) are strictly absent. Note that while cryptographic (SHA-256) and perceptual hashes verify file uniqueness and image differentiation, they do not capture fine spatial autocorrelation between adjacent tiles on the same wood block; this spatial proximity is explicitly eliminated in the strict specimen-disjoint benchmark. In sharp contrast, under the strict specimen-disjoint split in 19-class mode ($N_{\text{pairs}} = 1,204 \times 3,888 = 4,681,152$ cross-split comparisons; or $N_{\text{pairs}} = 1,060 \times 3,460 = 3,667,600$ in 17-class zero-leakage mode), the mean pairwise Hamming distance rises to $d_H = 26.85 \pm 5.48$, the count of near-duplicate pairs with $H \le 4$ drops to strictly $0$ ($0.00\%$), and the minimum observed cross-split Hamming distance is $H_{\min} = 11$, quantitatively confirming complete perceptual, morphological, and specimen independence across all 17 multi-specimen taxa.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Governed Benchmark Partitioning for Severe Specimen Scarcity}: In real-world CITES forensic xylaria, acquiring extensive physical specimen cohorts for endangered timbers is legally and ecologically constrained ($|\mathcal{G}_c| \le 10$ blocks per taxon; e.g., specifically 1 verified physical block accession each for \textit{Dalbergia cochinchinensis} and \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma}). Enforcing rigid whole-specimen isolation across 3-way splits would mathematically exclude rare single-specimen taxa from evaluation subsets ($\text{CCR} < 100\%$, minority class starvation), preventing customs officers and downstream models from verifying these critical species. To resolve this dilemma, ForensicMacroWood-CITES implements an operational canonical partition ($N_{\text{train}} = 3,959$, $N_{\text{val}} = 1,265$, $N_{\text{test}} = 1,190$) guaranteeing 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($\text{CCR} = 100.0\%$, 19/19 taxa) across all subsets with controlled boundary sharing ($\text{SLR} = 30.61\%$, Table~\ref{tab:split_allocation}), accompanied by a strict specimen-disjoint benchmark ($N_{\text{train}} = 3,888$, $N_{\text{val}} = 1,322$, $N_{\text{test}} = 1,204$ in 19-class mode; or $N_{\text{train}} = 3,460$, $N_{\text{val}} = 1,180$, $N_{\text{test}} = 1,060$ with $\text{SLR} = 0.0\%$ across all 17 multi-specimen taxa in zero-leakage mode, Table~\ref{tab:split_disjoint_allocation}).
\end{enumerate}

Standardized $224 \times 224$ px images are normalized during training using ImageNet channel statistics ($\boldsymbol{\mu} = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406]$, $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]$). Training augmentations include random resized crops (scale $0.8$--$1.0$), random horizontal and vertical flips ($p = 0.5$), mild color jitter (factors of $0.25$, hue $0.05$), and subtle grayscale conversion ($p = 0.05$).

\subsection{Technical Validation}
\label{sec:technical_validation}

To verify the technical usability, integrity, and reproducibility of the released dataset, metadata tables, and partition manifests, supervised technical-validation baseline experiments were conducted using the modern pure-convolutional architecture ConvNeXt-Tiny~\cite{convnext} trained under Multiclass Focal Loss. In accordance with the publication mandate of \textit{Data in Brief}, these baseline experiments serve strictly to confirm dataset functionality, validate dual-partition behaviors, and establish a turnkey reference benchmark for timber forensics. The accompanying repository code is modularized to support alternative backbones (such as ResNet-50~\cite{resnet} and EfficientNet-B0~\cite{efficientnet}) via standardized command-line flags.

Inspection of the multi-seed averaged test-set confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrix}, aggregated across three independent random initializations with cell annotations reporting rounded mean integer test counts alongside corresponding mean row-normalized recall percentages) reveals critical taxonomic patterns and practical forensic implications. Crucially, the lowest-performing classes in fine-grained multi-class classification are legally regulated under CITES Appendix~II: \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} (mean recall $2.5\%$, 1/40 captures), \textit{Afzelia quanzensis} ($57.1\%$, 32/56 captures), and \textit{Afzelia africana} ($58.6\%$, 34/58 captures). 

These classification errors follow two distinct patterns with divergent forensic consequences:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Intra-Genus Sister-Taxon Confusion (Benign Regulatory Error)}: 21 test captures of \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} ($52.5\%$) are misclassified as its congeneric sister taxon \textit{Afzelia bella}. Because both species are legally controlled under CITES Appendix~II with identical trade restrictions (Annotation \#17), misidentifying \textit{A. pachyloba} as \textit{A. bella} does not compromise regulatory enforcement at border checkpoints---the consignment remains correctly intercepted under CITES scrutiny. Furthermore, this machine learning confusion mirrors human expert difficulty: all 87 initial disputes between certified wood anatomists during independent label verification (Section~\ref{sec:inter_rater}) were localized between \textit{A. pachyloba} and \textit{A. bella} ($75.0\%$ of all \textit{A. pachyloba} captures), confirming extreme transverse anatomical convergence between these two taxa.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Genus Regulatory Misclassification (Critical Customs False Negative)}: In contrast, 24 test captures of \textit{Afzelia quanzensis} ($42.9\%$) are misclassified as \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma} (Non-CITES), and 13 captures of \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} ($32.5\%$) are predicted as \textit{Guibourtia ehie} (Non-CITES). These errors represent true forensic false negatives, where protected timber could erroneously pass border screening as unregulated commercial wood. These false negative errors stem from specific anatomical and pigmentation overlaps: for \textit{A. quanzensis} misclassified as \textit{G. coleosperma} (24 captures), both taxa exhibit reddish-brown heartwood, solitary/paired diffuse-porous vessels ($120$--$180\,\mu\text{m}$), and aliform parenchyma sheaths; whereas misclassifications of \textit{A. pachyloba} into \textit{G. ehie} (13 captures) are driven by \textit{G. ehie}'s pronounced dark vertical zebra-striping and wavy tangential parenchyma resembling transverse textural features of pale \textit{A. pachyloba} heartwood. The remaining false negatives comprise 3 captures of \textit{D. oliveri} $\to$ \textit{P. macrocarpus}, 2 captures of \textit{P. erinaceus} $\to$ \textit{P. indicus}, and 2 captures of \textit{P. soyauxii} $\to$ \textit{P. macrocarpus}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Binary CITES vs.\ Non-CITES Screening Performance}:
To evaluate practical utility for frontline customs triage where the immediate operational objective is flagging regulated timber shipments, we aggregated multi-class predictions into a binary forensic diagnostic framework (CITES Appendix~II Positive vs.\ Non-CITES Negative, where 11 species comprising 692 test captures represent regulated CITES timber, and 8 species comprising 498 test captures represent unregulated commercial controls):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{CITES Sensitivity (Recall)}: $\mathbf{93.64\%}$ (648 of 692 true CITES test captures correctly flagged as regulated; 44 false negatives).
    \item \textbf{Non-CITES Specificity}: $\mathbf{97.99\%}$ (488 of 498 non-regulated commercial captures correctly classified as non-CITES; only 10 false positive flags: 7 \textit{P. indicus} $\to$ \textit{P. erinaceus}, 1 \textit{S. tonkinensis} $\to$ \textit{D. tonkinensis}, and 2 \textit{S. tonkinensis} $\to$ \textit{P. erinaceus}).
    \item \textbf{CITES Precision (PPV)}: $\mathbf{98.48\%}$ (648 of 658 positive flags correspond to genuine CITES timber; false positive rate $= 2.01\%$, preventing costly unjustified consignment seizures).
    \item \textbf{Overall Binary Accuracy}: $\mathbf{95.46\%}$ ($1{,}136 / 1{,}190$ test samples correctly triaged; Binary F1-Score $= \mathbf{96.00\%}$).
\end{itemize}
This diagnostic evaluation confirms that while fine-grained 19-class discrimination remains challenging for minority sister taxa on the transverse plane alone, computer-vision macro-imaging functions as a high-throughput frontline triage tool ($93.64\%$ sensitivity, $97.99\%$ specificity). Consignments triggering border flags can subsequently undergo definitive confirmatory testing via laboratory microtomy or mass spectrometry.

As documented in Table~\ref{tab:split_comparison_baseline}, evaluating the ConvNeXt-Tiny baseline on the strict specimen-disjoint benchmark yields a mean overall accuracy of $82.35\% \pm 0.52\%$ and a macro-averaged F1-score of $76.92\% \pm 0.61\%$. Contrasted against the operational canonical benchmark ($90.42\% \pm 0.38\%$ accuracy, $86.80\% \pm 0.45\%$ macro-F1), this reveals an empirical generalization gap of $\Delta \text{Acc} = -8.07\%$ and $\Delta \text{F1} = -9.88\%$.

This performance difference is driven by multiple interacting factors: (i) inter-individual biological variance across unseen logs (natural variations in vessel diameter, axial parenchyma band frequency, and heartwood pigmentation influenced by tree ontogeny, soil chemistry, and microclimate); (ii) complete absence of intra-specimen spatial cues across partition boundaries; and (iii) slight shifts in class sample proportions between splits. Rather than asserting a single speculative mechanism, the benchmark establishes these two reference figures to reflect two distinct operational realities: front-line screening of timber lots where reference archives may overlap ($90.42\%$), and out-of-distribution deployment on entirely novel timber harvests ($82.35\%$).

\subsection{Limitations and Practical Considerations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several practical limitations and methodological trade-offs should be considered when utilizing the ForensicMacroWood-CITES benchmark:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Modest Physical Specimen Cohort Sizes}: Physical reference cohorts remain modest ($|\mathcal{G}_c| \le 10$ wood blocks per species, aggregating 147 verified physical specimens across the 19 Fabaceae taxa), with acute scarcity bottlenecks for strictly protected or rare commercial timbers (specifically 1 physical block accession for \textit{Dalbergia cochinchinensis} and \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma}). Consequently, intra-specific anatomical variation arising from geographic provenance, tree age, and micro-climatic growth conditions is not exhaustively sampled.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Specimen Allocation vs. Absolute Disjointness}: The operational canonical benchmark prioritizes a 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($\text{CCR} = 100.0\%$) across all subsets via controlled specimen boundary sharing ($\text{SLR} = 30.61\%$, 45 shared block instances out of 147 blocks). While cryptographic and perceptual hashing confirm that duplicate files are strictly identified, this partition is an operational screening compromise to prevent minority-class omission rather than an out-of-distribution generalization test. Researchers evaluating generalization across novel physical logs must utilize the provided specimen-disjoint split.
    \item \textbf{Laboratory Surface Preparation vs. In-the-Field Generalization}: All benchmark captures were recorded on orthogonally planed and progressively polished cross-sections (P120--P600 abrasives) under calibrated daylight-balanced ($5600\,\text{K}$) diffuse circular LED lighting. Reported baseline classification accuracy ($90.42\%$) may not directly generalize to rough chainsaw cuts, weathered end-grain surfaces, sawdust-obscured vessels, or uncontrolled solar glare encountered during real-time inspections at port container terminals or log landings.
    \item \textbf{Single Anatomical Plane}: The current benchmark focuses exclusively on the transverse (cross-sectional end-grain) plane. While the transverse view exposes primary diagnostic xylotomical systems (vessels, axial parenchyma halos, and growth increments), incorporating longitudinal radial and tangential surfaces~\cite{rosadasilva2022} in future releases could provide complementary discriminatory features for challenging sister species with convergent cross-sectional anatomy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}

The authors confirm adherence to ethical publication standards. This work does not involve human participants, animal experimentation, or personal data mining. The dataset consists strictly of non-destructive macroscopic optical digital photographs of dry, surfaced reference wood blocks; no living wild plant material was collected, and no genetic material or biochemical extracts were sampled, extracted, or sequenced. Consequently, this study does not utilize genetic resources and does not trigger access-and-benefit-sharing (ABS) compliance obligations under the Nagoya Protocol.

Regarding timber species listed in CITES Appendix~II (genera \textit{Afzelia}, \textit{Dalbergia}, and \textit{Pterocarpus}), all examined reference wood blocks originate from cataloged institutional xylarium reference archives and verified non-commercial academic forestry collections curated at the Forest Resources and Environment Center and IC4SD Laboratory in Viet Nam. These historical reference specimens were acquired in compliance with national forestry laws prior to the entry into force of CoP17 and CoP19 commercial trade restrictions or through institutional scientific specimen transfer protocols exclusively for non-commercial taxonomic authentication and educational purposes. All physical specimens remain curated in permanent institutional reference collections with traceable voucher accession codes recorded in \texttt{metadata/metadata.csv} under \texttt{specimen\_id}.
