\begin{abstract}
Wood species identification from macroscopic images plays a critical role in timber forensics, legal supply-chain verification, and enforcing international trade agreements such as CITES. While computer vision models offer rapid and non-destructive identification, their empirical evaluation frequently suffers from unaddressed data structure artifacts. In field-collected datasets, multiple observations (images) are routinely derived from the same physical wood specimen across various surface cuts, lighting conditions, or capture sessions. When datasets are partitioned using conventional random image-level splits, observations originating from the identical physical specimen contaminate both training and test partitions. Consequently, model performance metrics reflect memorization of source-specific background cues rather than generalizability to unseen biological specimens.

To address this foundational challenge, this paper introduces the \emph{Specimen-Centric Data Protocol} (SCDP), a general data governance framework for image tasks characterized by repeated physical observations. SCDP establishes the physical specimen as the primary unit of analysis, mandating traceable provenance metadata, hierarchical data structures, rigorous pre-split auditing, and specimen-disjoint partitioning. Built upon this framework, we formulate \emph{Class-wise Embedding-Guided Specimen Split} (CEGS-Split), which enforces specimen-level isolation, maintains per-species balance, and uses frozen feature embeddings to audit cross-split pairs for subtle source leakage. Evaluated on the benchmark S3 wood dataset comprising 18 species across 5 genera, our experiments show a notable performance degradation when transitioning from random image splits to specimen-disjoint splits, establishing that standard image-level benchmarks severely overestimate real-world capability. Furthermore, we establish a standardized benchmark comparing fine-grained classification baselines, core metric learning objectives, and self-supervised algorithms on held-out specimens, highlighting the essential role of auditable data protocols in scientific machine learning.
\end{abstract}
