\section{Results}

To evaluate specimen-level data leakage, we structure our empirical evaluation into two controlled settings:
\begin{itemize}
\item **Experiment I (Controlled Leakage Isolation)**: Evaluates frozen Swin-Large feature representations under a K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classifier to isolate the exact empirical inflation produced by dataset partitioning flaws.
\item **Experiment II (Deployable Baseline Diagnostics)**: Evaluates end-to-end fine-tuned ConvNeXt-Tiny models trained with Focal Loss on held-out physical specimens to characterize per-species diagnostic bottlenecks.
\end{itemize}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:split_ablation}, standard random image-level partitioning yields an inflated mean KNN accuracy of \textbf{95.81\%} and macro-F1 of \textbf{94.93\%}. Because feature representations, distance metrics, and classifier hyperparameters are strictly controlled across all trials, this \textbf{4.94 pp performance gap} isolates the exact empirical overestimation caused by specimen-level data leakage.

Crucially, the macro-F1 drop ($-6.35$ pp) is steeper than the accuracy drop ($-4.94$ pp), proving that specimen leakage artificially inflates metrics most severely on difficult minority classes, masking true biological classification error.

Note that overall accuracy in Experiment II ($80.28\%$) is lower than Experiment I ($90.87\%$). This difference stems from pipeline parameters: Experiment I utilizes frozen Swin-Large embeddings pre-trained on ImageNet-22k ($197\text{M}$ parameters) evaluated via KNN, whereas Experiment II evaluates ConvNeXt-Tiny ($28.6\text{M}$ parameters) fine-tuned end-to-end from scratch on the domain dataset under Focal Loss.

Evaluating on held-out physical specimens in Experiment II exposes diagnostic vulnerabilities across genera:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High Distinctiveness}: Species with distinct gross anatomical traits, such as \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma} (F1 = \textbf{0.9930}) and \textit{Dalbergia melanoxylon} (F1 = \textbf{0.9815}), achieve near-perfect classification on unseen specimens.
\item \textbf{Intra-Genus Confusion Bottlenecks}: Taxonomically close sibling species exhibit severe confusion. Within the \textit{Afzelia} genus, \textit{Afzelia bella} collapses to an F1-score of \textbf{0.0260} (support = 40 images across held-out physical specimen blocks) and \textit{Afzelia pachyloba} drops to \textbf{0.2708}. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Selection and Ranking Inversion under Data Leakage}
A central question in benchmark design is whether data leakage merely shifts absolute accuracy values or if it fundamentally distorts relative model rankings during architectural selection. Table~\ref{tab:ranking_inversion} compares four representative vision backbones evaluated under Random Image Split (R-Split) versus CEGS-Split (specimen-disjoint).

\begin{table}[htbp]

Under Random Image Split, **Swin-Large** appears superior ($96.42\%$ accuracy, Rank 1) due to its high parameter capacity ($197\text{M}$) to memorize high-dimensional background shortcuts. However, under specimen-disjoint governance (CEGS-Split), **ConvNeXt-Tiny** achieves higher true generalization ($91.24\%$ accuracy, Rank 1), outperforming Swin-Large.

This empirical rank inversion ($\rho_s = 0.40$) proves that random image-level splitting distorts architectural selection: published model leaderboards evaluated under leaked splits cannot be trusted to guide real-world deployment decisions.

\subsection{Key Methodological Findings}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Data Partitioning Dominates Model Innovation}: The \textbf{4.94 pp accuracy inflation} produced by random splitting exceeds the standard 1--3 pp accuracy gains typically claimed by new network backbones or loss functions in wood recognition literature.
\item \textbf{Leakage Distorts Architectural Rankings}: Random image splitting causes model ranking inversion ($\rho_s = 0.40$), favoring models that memorize shortcuts over models with true generalization.
\item \textbf{Leakage Masks Real-World Diagnostic Failure}: Random image splits conceal severe intra-genus confusion (such as in \textit{Afzelia}), providing false assurance of model readiness for CITES border customs deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
While this study provides controlled empirical evidence of specimen leakage, several methodological limitations should be noted:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Single-Dataset Scope}: Empirical validation was conducted on the 18-species S3 macroscopic wood benchmark. Evaluating SCDP across additional physical specimen domains (such as medical radiology, metallography, and leaf pathology) remains an important direction for future research.
\item \textbf{Single-Partition Evaluation}: Controlled leakage comparison was evaluated on a single locked CEGS-Split manifest. Evaluating multi-fold Leave-One-Specimen-Group-Out Cross-Validation is recommended when specimen counts permit.
\item \textbf{Sample Support on Rare Species}: Rare taxonomically difficult species (such as \textit{Afzelia bella}, support = 40 images) have limited physical specimen block counts, which contributes to high variance in per-species F1-scores.
\item \textbf{External Domain Shift}: This study isolates pure specimen leakage under matched acquisition conditions. External domain shift (e.g., cross-device camera variations across different customs ports) represents an additional orthogonal factor to be evaluated in future work.
\end{enumerate}
