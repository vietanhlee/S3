\section{Representation Analysis and Interpretability}

\subsection{Embedding Visualization}
We use t-SNE~\cite{tsne} to examine embeddings for the \textit{Afzelia} genus, comprising \textit{A.~africana}, \textit{A.~bella}, \textit{A.~pachyloba}, and \textit{A.~quanzensis}. This genus is selected because classification performance is uneven and the species are taxonomically close. Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne_comparison} compares the embedding space of the classification baseline against that of a representation learning model.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/tsne_comparison.png}
\caption{t-SNE projection of embeddings for the \textit{Afzelia} genus under the baseline and representation learning models.}
\label{fig:tsne_comparison}
\end{figure}

t-SNE is an exploratory projection; distances and densities in the two-dimensional plane depend on hyperparameters and do not faithfully preserve the geometry of the original space. Visible clusters in the figure must not be used for split selection, checkpoint selection, or conclusions about separability. Quantitative conclusions are drawn from retrieval metrics, NMI, and clustering indices computed in the original embedding space on the locked dataset.

\subsection{Distance Distribution}
Fig.~\ref{fig:distance_distribution} shows the distribution of Euclidean distances between same-species and different-species pairs. For $L_2$-normalized embeddings, this distance is directly related to cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
\|v_i-v_j\|_2=\sqrt{2(1-v_i^\top v_j)}.
\end{equation}
A useful model tends to reduce intra-class distances and increase inter-class distances, but the two distributions may overlap for taxonomically close species or specimens acquired under different conditions. The plot is used to identify overlap regions and flag pairs for inspection, not to assert an absolute classification boundary.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/distance_distribution.png}
\caption{Euclidean distance distributions between same-species and different-species pairs in embedding space.}
\label{fig:distance_distribution}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Attention Maps and Shortcut Checks}
Grad-CAM and Finer-CAM are used to qualitatively inspect the image regions that contribute to model predictions~\cite{gradcam}. Fig.~\ref{fig:grad_cam} shows attention maps from the classification baseline alongside those from a model trained with a metric learning objective.

For the classification baseline, attention maps occasionally show diffuse activation or anomalous focus on image borders, saw marks, or surface processing artifacts---signals with no taxonomic relevance. In contrast, models trained with Deep Metric Learning (Triplet Loss, Supervised Contrastive) or SSL objectives (Barlow Twins, BYOL) tend to produce more focused activation on anatomically meaningful structures recognized by wood identification experts: vessel pores, axial parenchyma bands, and wood ray orientation. This qualitative pattern is consistent with a learned representation that captures transferable anatomical features rather than acquisition-specific artifacts.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig/grad-cam.png}
\caption{Example Grad-CAM and Finer-CAM maps for qualitative inspection of prediction-driving regions.}
\label{fig:grad_cam}
\end{figure}

Attention maps are not causal proof that a model uses a specific anatomical feature, and they do not substitute for expert wood anatomy assessment. Their value within SCDP is as an audit support tool: combined with the manifest, they help detect whether a model has learned shortcuts introduced by imaging device characteristics, capture conditions, or preprocessing. A more rigorous evaluation requires occlusion experiments, counterfactual interventions, and cross-device or cross-source generalization tests.
