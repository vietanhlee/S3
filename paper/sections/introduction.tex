\section{Introduction}

Automated visual screening systems powered by deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs) have emerged as essential computational tools across physical science domains, including botanical identification, radiograph interpretation, agricultural crop diagnostic screening, and materials characterization~\cite{woodreview,rosadasilva2022,varoquaux2022,roberts2021}. In forestry science and international timber trade regulation, rapid macroscopic wood species identification is critical for enforcing international trade compliance under CITES Appendix II regulations~\cite{cites}, verifying legal timber supply chains, preventing illegal deforestation, and supporting forensic forestry investigations~\cite{dormontt2015,song2025,liu2025}. Computer vision systems promise field-deployable, non-destructive verification that can supplement labor-intensive xylotomy procedures in remote customs checkpoints. However, despite reported classification accuracies exceeding 95\% to 99\% across recent literature, computer vision models deployed on physical specimen imagery frequently suffer from catastrophic performance degradation when evaluated in operational field environments.

We argue that a primary driver of this systemic deployment failure is that dataset creation and evaluation workflows in applied computer vision remain largely ad-hoc, unstandardized, and methodologically unverified. We formalize this fundamental computational challenge under a unified paradigm: \textbf{Physical Entity-Centric Visual Classification (PECVC)}. PECVC encompasses any visual recognition domain where individual physical entities---such as biological wood logs, patient tissue blocks, agricultural crop specimens, or alloy metal samples---generate multiple correlated image observations.

In prevailing PECVC workflows, researchers harvest multiple high-resolution images from physical objects, store them in flat directory structures organized solely by target class labels, and immediately partition datasets into training and evaluation sets using conventional \emph{random image-level splits}. Crucially, this workflow ignores physical object provenance. When random image-level partitioning is applied to PECVC datasets, sub-images or cropped patches originating from the exact same physical entity are unintentionally distributed across both training and test sets.

This systemic flaw induces a subtle but impactful failure mode formally conceptualized in plant vision literature as \textbf{Same-Specimen-Picture Bias (SSPB)}~\cite{figueroamata2022} and more broadly recognized as \textbf{specimen-level data leakage}~\cite{varoquaux2022,roberts2021}. Under random image-level partitioning, deep neural networks act as opportunistic "shortcut learners" or "Clever Hans" predictors~\cite{geirhos2020,lapuschkin2019}. Rather than learning class-discriminative biological or anatomical features (such as vessel pore distributions, axial parenchyma arrangements, and ray parenchyma widths), deep networks exploit non-taxonomic surface artifacts, mechanical saw marks, optical illumination gradients, and camera sensor noise fingerprints unique to individual physical specimens. Consequently, reported high test accuracy does not measure the model's capacity to recognize novel physical samples; instead, it measures its capacity to re-identify previously seen physical sources.

\subsection{Mechanisms of Specimen-Level Data Leakage}
To understand why random image splitting causes severe performance overestimation, we formalize three distinct visual and physical mechanisms through which specimen leakage manifests in deep vision models:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Mechanism \arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Geometric and Anatomical Continuity}: Multiple sub-images or cropped patches extracted from the same physical wood block share continuous anatomical trajectories, including matching growth ring curvatures, identical vessel pore cluster arrangements, and continuous axial parenchyma bands. Deep networks memorize these spatial configurations as high-dimensional visual fingerprints.

\item \textbf{Acquisition and Illumination Fingerprints}: Images captured during the same photographic session inherit identical camera sensor noise patterns (Photo-Response Non-Uniformity, PRNU), localized specular flash reflections, optical lens vignetting gradients, white balance color casts, and fixed background shadows. The network learns camera acquisition conditions rather than biological species features.

\item \textbf{Surface Preparation and Mechanical Artifacts}: Physical specimens undergo mechanical preparation (saw cutting, planing, sanding). Micro-scratches from saw blade teeth, sanding grit scratch density, varnish/stain absorption variations, radial weathering cracks, and edge discoloration are unique to each specimen block. These non-taxonomic surface marks act as shortcut features for class prediction~\cite{geirhos2020}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Taxon Heterogeneity and The No Free Lunch Theorem in Data Partitioning}
While standard group-based splitting methods (such as \texttt{GroupKFold} or \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}) enforce physical specimen isolation, they suffer from two fundamental limitations:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Feature-Space Blindness}: Physical group isolation does not prevent two \emph{distinct} physical specimens with identical surface textures, sanding grit scratch patterns, or optical vignetting from being placed in separate splits, creating subtle visual feature-space leakage.
\item \textbf{Taxon Heterogeneity \& The No Free Lunch Theorem}: Wood species exhibit highly divergent intra-species biological variation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Uniform Species}: Taxa with homogeneous growth rings and consistent color density (e.g., \textit{Guibourtia coleosperma}) are cleanly partitioned by linear centroid distance metrics such as Mahalanobis distance or Ward agglomerative clustering.
\item \textbf{Outlier-Prone Species}: Taxa exhibiting extreme heartwood/sapwood color transitions or severe weathering abrasions (e.g., \textit{Afzelia bella}) distort standard distance metrics, requiring Adversarial MLP Discriminator validation or DataSAIL Integer Linear Programming (ILP) to isolate anomalous specimens.
\item \textbf{Visually Similar Sibling Species}: Taxa belonging to the same genus (e.g., \textit{Dalbergia oliveri} vs. \textit{Dalbergia tonkinensis}) exhibit high inter-specimen similarity, requiring Cosine Graph spectral component isolation to sever indirect leakage.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

Fixing a single splitting algorithm globally across all 18 species forces a sub-optimal partition for taxa whose structural distributions violate the algorithm's assumptions. Allowing each taxon $c \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ ($C=18$) to independently select its optimal splitting protocol $m_c \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ ($K=11$ candidate solvers) yields a combinatorial configuration space of:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} = K^C = 11^{18} \approx 5.5599 \times 10^{18} \quad \text{candidate partitions.}
\end{equation}

Evaluating all $11^{18}$ candidate datasets by brute-force search is computationally impossible (requiring over $176$ million CPU years). Furthermore, global metrics such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy ($MMD$) and Inter-Class Hard Negative F1-scores must be evaluated on the **assembled global dataset** $\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{m}) = \bigcup_{c=1}^C \mathcal{D}_c(m_c)$.

\subsection{Research Questions and Primary Contributions}
This paper addresses four central research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Data Governance Framework}: How can physical acquisition provenance, immutable manifest schemas, and cross-split proximity auditing be unified into a standardized data governance framework?
\item \textbf{Benchmarked Wood Dataset Creation}: How can a macro cross-sectional wood dataset be curated and partitioned to preserve 100\% subfolder integrity and 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($CCR = 100.0\%$)?
\item \textbf{Empirical Quantification of Leakage Inflation}: What is the exact quantitative margin ($\Delta \text{Acc}$, $\Delta \text{F1}$) by which naive random image splits overestimate performance compared to strict specimen-disjoint partitions?
\item \textbf{Multi-Objective Combinatorial Meta-Selection}: How can Simulated Annealing be formulated over the $11^{18}$ search space to discover Pareto-optimal global partitions balancing leakage reduction, OOD difficulty, and hard class accuracy?
\end{enumerate}

The primary contributions of this work are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Contribution 1: Specimen-Centric Data Protocol (SCDP) Framework}: We formalize a comprehensive data governance framework covering physical acquisition logging, immutable JSON/CSV metadata schemas, group-disjoint partitioning, and cross-split feature proximity auditing.
\item \textbf{Contribution 2: Curated \& Governed S3 Wood Dataset (18 Taxa)}: We present a benchmarked macroscopic cross-sectional wood dataset of 18 botanical species spanning 5 genera, fully annotated with physical specimen IDs, guaranteeing 100\% subfolder integrity ($SLR = 0.0\%$) and 100\% Class Coverage Rate ($CCR = 100.0\%$).
\item \textbf{Contribution 3: Multi-Objective Simulated Annealing Meta-Selector ($\text{CEGS-Split}$)}: We introduce a meta-heuristic optimization engine operating over an $11^{18}$ configuration space to select class-wise optimal splitting strategies evaluated against a global multi-objective fitness function.
\item \textbf{Contribution 4: Systematic 6-Protocol Leakage Benchmark (16 Metrics)}: On the S3 dataset, we evaluate a streamlined benchmark of 6 representative splitting protocols across 16 quantitative metrics, proving that naive random image splitting inflates accuracy to $99.87\%$ ($SLR = 100.0\%$), whereas DataSAIL ILP minimizes leakage loss to $4,841,999.8$ ($SLR = 5.7\%$, $MMD = 0.1199$) and Multi-Objective Meta-Selection achieves the highest hardest-class F1-score ($0.8148$).
\end{itemize}
